## How strongly does this read as an AI-generated story?

`One Bad Year` reads strongly as an AI-generated story. In the very first sentence, our writer uses a very common scene description pattern seen in LLM-generated stories. This pattern often uses **smell**, **taste** or **scent** followed by sentence modifiers. 

`Jebediah woke with a gasp that tasted of old copper and dry earth.` 

`One Bad Year` repeats many AI writing tics throughout the story. Contrastive framing is very prominent. We can find repeated words and phrases that are used in similar or identical contexts.

>Note! Below are samples, not the full list of AI tics found in One Bad Year. Some of the examples display more than one tic in a sentence. So, you may see contrastive framing in the same sentence with modifiers. 

**Contrastive framing**:

- Scene 1: "**He wasn't** in the ruins. **He was** lying in his own bed, the familiar sagging mattress supporting his weathered frame."
- Scene 1: "**He didn't see** the wasteland; **he saw** a puzzle he finally knew how to solve."
- Scene 4: "**It didn't arrive** as a cloud; **it arrived** as a wall of absolute midnight, a towering monolith of **grit** and **sulfur** that erased the sun in a **single**, **heartbeat**-shudder."
- Scene 4: "The wind **didn't just blow**; **it hammered**."

**Triplet framing**:

- Scene 1: "He felt the ghostly phantom of New Year's Eve—**the cold**, **the desperation**, **the crushing knowledge** that everything was gone—clinging to his skin like a second layer of grime."

**Sensory language in scene descriptions**:

>Note! In the samples below, the sensory words are highlighted in italics, while the modifiers are in bold.

- Scene 1: "Jebediah woke with a gasp that *tasted* of **old copper** and **dry earth.**"
- Scene 4: "For hours, they sat in the suffocating dark, the air thick with the *taste* of **old copper** and **alkaline salt**."

**Quirks and questionable decisions**:

- Scene 2: "...But as he stepped back out into the oppressive silence of the dying town, he realized the terrible truth. He could predict the storm, he could even see the banker's fear, but the ink in the ledger was already dry. **Knowing the future was a ghost's privilege; it didn't mean he had the power to stop the wind.**"
>Comment! This might be my preference, but the text in bold feels like a conclusion. It feels unnecessary. We know that a person can't stop the wind.

- Scene 4: "He threw himself against the wall, his hands clawing at the boards as if he could physically hold the house together. But the environmental determinism of the plains was absolute. **The storm didn't care about his memories or his maps. It was a macro-climatic machine, and he was merely a speck of dust within its gears.**"
>Comment! I don't feel good about the text highlighted in bold. Of course, the storm doesn't care about Jeb. The storm is not a living being; it cannot care.

- Scene 6: "I failed you. I failed **the children**." 
>Comment! We never see kids in this story. As a matter of fact, nobody even mentions them until this sentence.

## Opinion

>Note! The following opinion is for One Bad Year as it was outlined in Open Hollywood's pre-production and later created in production after blind human approval. 

`One Bad Year` doesn't start badly, although I would've preferred a bit more shock when Jebediah wakes up and realizes that the entire year has been reset. Jebediah never checks his livestock or his equipment. Perhaps in January of 1934 he had a different number of cows or chickens. But we never experience these subtle clues. We just see the calendar.

Then, Jebediah does something unusual: he goes to the bank to tell the banker, Silas Vane, that the wheat price will slide ten cents a bushel before noon. People go to the bank to get cash, pay bills, get a loan, etc. I've never heard of a common man going there to tell the bankers about commodity prices going down. We can argue about why Jebediah did that, but I don't see this as a good fit for the story.

`One Bad Year` is a difficult setup for a short story, but our writer makes questionable decisions, fluffs the story with filler, and opens weak subplots that are never addressed later on.

Jebediah appears in every scene, but after reading the entire story, I don't know much about him. Aside from being a married farmer, who is he? What makes him tick? What's important to him? What do we know about his wife Clara, except that she loves her husband?

Sometimes we get difficult tasks and could explore thinking outside of the box. Our writer never did that.
