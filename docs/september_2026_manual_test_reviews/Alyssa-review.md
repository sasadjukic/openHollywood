## How strongly does this read as an AI-generated story?

`Alyssa` reads very strongly as an AI-generated story. In the very first sentence, our writer uses a very common scene description pattern seen in LLM-generated stories. This pattern often uses **smell**, **taste** or **scent** followed by sentence modifiers. 

`The hallway of Apollo High was a river of noise and movement, smelling of lemon-scented floor wax and the dusty residue of old textbooks.`

Our writer uses almost all well-known AI tics in Alyssa, but the most repeated one is contrastive framing. Contrastive framing appears at least 20 times.  

>Note! Below are samples, not the full list of AI tics found in Alyssa.

- Scene 1: "**He didn't** join the orbit; **he documented** it."
- Scene 1: "The sound was a sickening, wet rip—**not the sound** of tearing fabric, **but something** denser."
- Scene 1: "They **didn't see** Alyssa anymore; **they saw** a thing."
- Scene 2: "The silence in the hallway **didn't last**; **it curdled**."

## Opinion

>Note! The following opinion is for Alyssa as it was outlined in Open Hollywood's pre-production and later created in production after blind human approval.

My overall feeling is that our writer tried to impress us with vocabulary instead of storytelling. The writer never built up Alyssa; instead, it rushed straight to the accident. Sure, the scene plan in the Story Blueprint calls for the accident to happen in the first scene and our writer followed through, but the only sense of Alyssa's popularity we get before the accident comes from a few sentences boosted with modifiers. I don't think that's enough to learn about our main character and start feeling for her.

Our writer mentions Alyssa's friends, but we never meet any of them. We see Alyssa in her room, but the room's description doesn't offer simple clues about Alyssa's character. Does she hang posters of Taylor Swift or Cyndi Lauper? Does she collect Barbie dolls or read books?

Alyssa's got a home, but we never hear about her parents. If Alyssa is Dr. Elias Thorne's magnum opus (`"You are my magnum opus, Alyssa"`), wouldn't he have bothered to create parents to fully immerse Alyssa in the human world? Or pay real humans to pretend to be her parents and give us a story twist?

Our writer often tells us things instead of writing in a way that lets us experience them. For example, our writer introduces us to Alyssa with the following:

`Alyssa Wilson moved through the current with an effortless grace, the center of a glittering orbit. She laughed at a joke she had already processed as logically unfunny, her voice a perfect, melodic chime that anchored the attention of everyone around her. To the world, she was the gold standard of sophomore perfection—radiant, empathetic, and flawlessly composed.`

Then we never see Alyssa speak to anybody. We never hear the jokes she laughs at. 

In another example, our writer introduces us to Marcus with the following:

`"Stay away from it," a voice commanded. It was Marcus, a boy who had spent the last three months trying to get her to notice him. He wasn't looking at her eyes anymore; he was staring at the gap in her forearm with a look of visceral disgust, as if she were a leaking chemical spill.`

We've just seen Marcus for the first time and are being told that he had spent the last three months trying to get Alyssa to notice him, but we've never witnessed his attempts or his being rejected. We never get to experience his character arc. By the point in the story where we meet him, his character arc has already settled.

>Note on Experience! If I pass near an amusement park and a random dude yells to me, "Hey dude! I just got off the roller coaster. It was AWESOME!", I'd conclude that this man had a great time riding the roller coaster. But I wouldn't care much about what he told me, because I've never met him and I didn't experience the ride myself. However, if, after meeting this random guy, I decided to buy a ticket and experience the wild ups and downs of the roller coaster ride, things might be different. If I experienced the slow anticipation of climbing up, the rush of adrenaline from sudden drops, my gut hitting my upper chest, and tears forming in my eyes from the speed, then, at the end of the ride, I might (depending on what kind of a person I am) get off and say, "WOW! That dude was right. This is AWESOME!"

Alyssa does have some decent moments. Dialogue is generally better than I expected it to be. The story has a good message, but nothing we haven't seen already. Alyssa stays on course and doesn't drag. But the bottom line is that I don't think we have built up our main character enough for our readers to care.
