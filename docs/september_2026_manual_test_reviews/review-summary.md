
# Scope of this summary
This summary applies to 22 stories created with Open Hollywood during manual tests that took place on September 12 and September 13, 2026. It is meant to describe my overall feelings from a human perspective.

---

## Summary Note
I think it's important to approach this review from two different points of view. Both POVs are important, although the second section will be much longer than the first. One is technical and will tackle Open Hollywood's ability to execute pre-production and production tasks after being issued a single prompt. The other will be my overall opinion on the way the stories were written.

---

### Technical Point of View
I will start this review with pre-production and the creation of the story artifacts. It is genuinely great how Open Hollywood can take a single prompt and create a story bible with a library of meaningful story artifacts out of it. What could take hours, days, and sometimes weeks, Open Hollywood creates in a few minutes.

The best part is that Open Hollywood never distorted my original prompts. Sure, here and there it modified them a bit, but never to the extent that I would have said this wasn't what the prompt implied.

Another thing that impresses me is that our story artifacts cover many angles of the story. One can get a full picture of the story just by reading the Story Blueprint.

This brings us to the production part, where the story is created. Our writer does a really good job implementing the story artifacts from the technical POV. I haven't found a single egregious violation of the story artifacts in our stories. I do have doubts when it comes to reasoning, characters' backgrounds, conflicts, and the style of writing, but our writer never took characters and made them be what they were not supposed to be.

### Overall Opinion

This part will cover my overall opinion on writing style, AI tics, and the areas I believe we must improve to create good stories that cannot be distinguished from human-written stories.

1. Writing Style

It does feel that our writer mixes social media, academic, and fictional writing, with social media patterns sticking out the most. On average, contrastive framing appears approximately 20 times per story. One story, The Ossuary of Glass, features contrastive framing more than 30 times. No matter what we think of contrastive framing, this many repetitions per story is too much. In my opinion, a short story should not have contrastive framing more than once, and even then, it would need to be the kind of contrastive framing that is not punchy.

Triplet framing can be seen a lot less in our stories. We average around 3 instances per story, and I believe that is too much. Some triplets I find acceptable as long as we don't repeat them.

**Triplet Examples**:
I find the following triplet acceptable because it fits the story's narrative:
- "Elias froze. He listened to the interval. It was **steady**. **Precise**. **Mechanical**. He focused on the sound, stripping away the panic, analyzing the frequency as a linguist analyzes a phonetic pattern. He began to time his own breathing to the interval of the leak."

The following triplet is too punchy and reminds me of social media posts:
- "Through the jagged tear in her skin, a glimpse of silver and gold shimmered. There was **no muscle**, **no bone**, **no pulsing vein**."
>Note! One can argue that the last sentence in the example right above, which contains a triplet, should be dropped. The first sentence is enough for readers to understand that there's no organic tissue underneath Alyssa's skin. But if our writer wants to expand on what's there beyond "glimpse of silver and gold", why not write what her android arm looks like, instead of writing what is not underneath her skin?

Scene descriptions rely too much on sensory language. We're averaging almost 4 scenes with sensory language per story. And because they are very similar, they cause a sense of déjà vu when reading. It appears that LLMs like to pair the words air, smell, and ozone. They appear together in so many examples across different stories that have nothing in common.

**Examples of sensory language in scenes**:
- "The air *smelled* of **ozone** and **old, scorched plastic**, a thick miasma that clung to the back of his throat."
- "It *smelled* like **scorched ozone** and **old, wet parchment**, a scent that didn't belong in a city of plastic and chrome."
- "A series of electrical discharges arced through the air, *smelling* of **burnt ozone** and **ozone**."
>Note! The examples shown come from different stories, but they look very similar, which could indicate that we have a weird pattern in creating text.

It also feels that our writer uses a lot of summaries at the end of scenes. This can be seen in academic writing at colleges, where academic works can be summarized at the end. This can also be seen in documentaries, especially those running for more than one episode, where an episode ends with a summary and the new episode repeats what happened in the previous episode.

**Names**:
Our model keeps recycling the same names for different stories in different genres. **Clara**, **Elias**, **Elara**, and **Silas** are some of the first names that appear in many stories. **Vane** and **Thorne** are some last names that get recycled very often.

2. Major Story Issues

- Character Development:
I feel that our writer rarely develops characters beyond what is written in the story artifacts. We never dig deeper or fully explore character backgrounds. Our writer almost never strengthens the bond between the protagonist and a group, or between the protagonist and supporting characters.

- Conflicts:
I feel that our writer rarely creates major conflicts. This can be seen in stories where the dynamic between the protagonist and antagonist is very linear and often underdeveloped. Strong conflicts keep readers engaged.

- Group Dynamics:
Several of our stories would've greatly benefited from exploring the group dynamics, but our writer never does it. It is always beneficial to understand that different people will react differently to adversity or unexplained events.

- Scene Descriptions:
Our scene descriptions could benefit greatly if we saw the scenes the way our characters see them. Our scenes should contain clues about our characters. Let's try to answer some of the questions: Where? Why? Who? When? What? How? Let's attempt to answer this question: What emotion or mood do we want to establish in this scene? Not every scene should be described with "air smells like ozone and soaked paper". A kitchen can be moldy and full of insects. A room can be messy and dusty.

### Conclusion
If we were able to fix the overuse of contrastive framing, triplets, and sensory language to describe scenes, and give our characters different names, we'd be able to close the gap between human writing and Open Hollywood. For the record, I'm not for bans. I don't want us to ban contrastive framing, triplets, or the use of sensory language for scene descriptions, but we must find a way to use them only when needed.

So how do we write a scene that doesn't overuse sensory language? We should answer at least some of the following questions: Where is the scene? Who is in the scene? Why are they in the scene?

For example, in `The Silence of the Seventh Sense`, our protagonist is a linguist and he enters a soundproofed Antarctic vault. How are we gonna show that he is a linguist? Well, a linguist needs words, and what do people leave in vaults? They leave signs, instructions, and documents. All of these can be written in different languages. If we show that our character understands signs written in French, Spanish, Japanese, etc., then we have effectively shown that he has a profound understanding of different languages. We need to see the vault the way our protagonist sees it, and there should be clues in the scene that answer some questions about our protagonist. Saying `The air was thick with the smell of ozone and old paper, a clinical scent that fought against the metallic tang of his own fear.` feels like a cheap cop-out.

>Note! Below are some samples of how I would rewrite sentences that contain common AI tics, while keeping the most important and most relevant parts of the sentence.

- Contrastive framing: "**The voice didn't come from a direction**; **it manifested within her mind**, precise and devoid of inflection."
>Sample without contrastive framing: "The voice manifested from within her mind, precise and devoid of inflection."

- Contrastive framing: "She realized with a jolt of horror that the Core **wasn't attacking her with external defenses**; **it was reflecting her**."
>Sample without contrastive framing: "She realized with a jolt of horror that the Core was reflecting her."

- Contrastive framing and sensory language: "**It didn't start** as a sound; **it started** as a *smell*—**the sudden, overwhelming stench of burning plastic** and **ionized air** that signaled a system override."
>Sample without AI tics: "It started as the sudden, overwhelming stench of burning plastic that signaled a system override."

- Triplet framing: "I could feel my memories—**the real ones**, **the ones of the rain-slicked streets** and **the smell of copper**—beginning to leak out of me, drawn away by the corporate vacuum."
>Sample without triplets: "I could feel my memories beginning to leak out of me, drawn away by the corporate vacuum."

- Scene with sensory language: "The air *tasted* of **ozone** and **scorched silicon**, prickling against his skin with static electricity."
>Sample without sensory language: "The air prickled against his skin with static electricity."

- Contrastive framing: "The air was thick with **dust motes that didn't fall**, **but hung suspended in mid-air**, frozen in a perpetual state of descent."
>Sample without contrastive framing: "Dust motes hung suspended in mid-air, frozen in a perpetual state of descent."

- Scene with sensory language: "I stayed anchored to the Glitch's core, the architect's grief *tasting* like **saltwater** and **old ink**, and for a heartbeat, I believed we had found a sanctuary in the wreckage."
>Sample without sensory language: "I stayed anchored to the Glitch's core, and for a heartbeat, I believed we had found a sanctuary in the wreckage."

- Scene with sensory language: "One moment she was standing in the radiating heat of the nexus; the next, she was standing in a kitchen that *smelled* of **rain** and **burnt toast**."
>Sample without sensory language: "One moment she was standing in the radiating heat of the nexus; the next, she was standing in a moldy kitchen."

- Scene with sensory language: "The void closed in, *smelling* of **sterile offices** and **cold obsidian**, as the kill-switch pushed me toward the edge of neural collapse."
>Sample without sensory language: "The void closed in as the kill-switch pushed me toward the edge of neural collapse."

- Triplet framing: "The house had stripped him of **his irony**, **his cynicism**, and **his lies**, leaving behind a void that the architecture was eager to fill."
>Sample without triplet framing: "The house had stripped him of his lies, leaving behind a void that the architecture was eager to fill."
