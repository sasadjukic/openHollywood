## How strongly does this read as an AI-generated story?

`The Ossuary of Glass` reads strongly as an AI-generated story. The story starts with contrastive framing.

`The coastal fog did not merely surround the estate; it seemed to exhale from the very soil, a thick, saline shroud that rendered the horizon an impossibility.`

Our writer repeats many well-known AI tics, with contrastive framing being omnipresent. It appears in the story at least 30 times.

>Note! Below are samples, not the full list of AI tics found in The Ossuary of Glass.

**Contrastive framing**:

- Scene 1: "The house **isn't a puzzle**; **it's a symptom**."
- Scene 1: "The sound **was not a mere closing**, **but a seal**."
- Scene 1: " As they stood there, the silence of the house deepened, and for the first time, the scholars realized that the fog outside **had not just followed them in**—**it had vanished**, leaving them enclosed in a space that no longer recognized the concept of an exit.
- Scene 2: "The silence that followed the disappearance of the doors **was not a void**, **but a presence**."

- Scene 5: "The house had stripped him of **his irony**, **his cynicism**, and **his lies**, leaving behind a void that the architecture was eager to fill."

## Opinion

>Note! The following opinion is for The Ossuary of Glass as it was outlined in Open Hollywood's pre-production and later created in production after blind human approval.

`The Ossuary of Glass` starts well, with one of our characters, Dr. Elias Thorne, explaining what the group is doing at the location (although our writer used contrastive framing):

`We are here to document the physical and historical reality of the estate, not to engage in sensory conjecture.`

The story flows at a decent pace, and our characters are starting to realize they are trapped. It is here that I would've liked to see a bit more of the group dynamics explored.

When a group of people experiences unknown danger, individuals react differently. One person wants to do one thing; another wants to do something differently, etc. This creates tension in the group. Tension usually splits the group, therefore weakening it. This kind of pattern and these group dynamics are often explored in horror stories.

Not all of our characters need to be "structurally integrated" at once. One character can "die" first, bringing additional tension and reactions. The tensions in a group reveal more about character backgrounds, and I don't think we've seen enough of that.

`The Ossuary of Glass` is a decent story, but could've been a really good suspenseful story if our writer had developed the group dynamic necessary for that to happen.
