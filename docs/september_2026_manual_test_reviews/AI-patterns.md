The purpose of this document is to put together the major patterns LLMs repeat when tasked with any form of creative writing. Humans now increasingly look for these patterns to recognize LLM-generated text.

>NOTE! Just for the record, I do not believe LLMs are AI. However, in everyday talk, media coverage, and for different marketing reasons, LLMs and AI are used as synonyms. So, in this report, when I say *AI story* or *AI pattern*, it means the same as *LLM story* or *LLM pattern*.

## AI Patterns

Human writers have tics. LLMs have their own tics. The problem is that the LLM tics are glaring and in your face. They happen so often that they become hard to ignore. Because AI writing is a fairly new thing, there's no consensus on how to brand these tics. So, for this purpose, I'll give my own names to these repetitive patterns. I'll provide examples of common AI patterns so we're clear about their meaning.

**Common AI patterns**

1. **Contrastive framing**

Contrastive framing is everywhere. It can often be seen on LinkedIn and X (formerly Twitter), heard in YouTube videos, and even heard in human speech. Occasionally, you may see it in written fiction, but LLM-generated texts love contrastive framing. It is one of the most recognizable patterns in LLM writing.

Contrastive framing, as seen on social media, comes structured in a sentence as displayed in the following examples:

- not **X**, but **Y**
- don't do **X**, do **Y**

Examples of contrastive framing from LLM-generated stories, where contrasts are highlighted in **bold**:

- Example 1: "**He didn't join** the orbit; **he documented** it."
- Example 2: "**They didn't see** Alyssa anymore; **they saw** a thing."
- Example 3: "The silence of the Maintenance Facility **was not** a natural quiet; **it was** the heavy, pressurized silence of a vacuum..."
- Example 4: "**It did not move**; **it thickened**."
- Example 5: "**We aren't people** anymore; **we're just currency**."

2. **Overuse of sensory language in scene descriptions**

It is true that many writing teachers recommend using sensory language to describe scenes in your fiction. It is true that human writers use it, but LLM-generated texts use too much of it. It appears that almost every scene must use one of the following to describe it: **sight**, **sound**, **smell**, **touch**, **taste**, or **scent**. What is even more interesting is that LLMs prefer to describe *air* with sensory language and add modifiers (specifically two modifiers) after a sensory trigger word is used.

A typical LLM-generated scene using sensory language comes in the following form:
- ... *sensory word* ... **modifier 1** and **modifier 2**
- ... *sensory word* ... **modifier 1**, **modifier 2**
- ... air ... *sensory word* ... **modifier 1** and **modifier 2**

Examples of scenes from LLM-generated text, where sensory words are highlighted in italics, while the modifiers are in bold:

- Example 1: "The air was thick with the *smell* of **ozone** and **old paper**, a clinical scent that fought against the metallic tang of his own fear."
- Example 2: "The vacuum snapped, and a rush of fresh, cold moorland air swept into the chamber, *smelling* of **rain** and **wild heather**."
- Example 3: "Here, the air was thick with the *scent* of **ozone** and **ancient, crushed minerals**, vibrating with a vascular humming that resonated in the marrow of her bones."
- Example 4: "The air *smelled* of **ozone** and **old memories**—the *scent* of a rain-drenched street from a decade ago, the smell of a woman's perfume that I couldn't  name, the metallic tang of a hospital ward."
- Example 5: "The air around him *smelled* of **old parchment** and **the sharp, metallic tang of ink**."

3. **Sibling framing** 

Sibling framing, often branded as triplet framing, or simply triplets, primarily uses parallelism and enumeration to achieve a punchy delivery or stress a point in a catchy way. It most commonly comes in triplets, but can sometimes come in twins.

There's nothing wrong with sibling framing. It appears often on social media and in human speech, but LLMs tend to overuse it and pack some stories with it. We can see it structured in a sentence like this:

- no **X**, no **Y**, no **Z**.
- **X**, **Y**, and **Z**.

Examples of sibling framing from LLM-generated text, where the siblings are highlighted in **bold**:

- Example 1: "There was **no muscle**, **no bone**, **no pulsing vein**."
- Example 2: "You're gonna get an AI that's **bitter**, **jaded**, and **probably bankrupt**."
- Example 3: "There were **no codes here**. **No shifts in shade**, **no calculated gaps in the satin stitch**."
- Example 4: "She opened her mouth to speak again, **to plead**, **to scream**, **to tell them** that she couldn't bear the weight of their silence any longer."
- Example 5: "The house had stripped him of **his irony**, **his cynicism**, and **his lies**, leaving behind a void that the architecture was eager to fill."

4. **Weird metaphors and similes**: Metaphors and similes are part of literature, but LLMs generate random metaphors that sometimes have nothing to do with the context of the story.

- Example 1: “Prisoners,” he announced, “you stand aboard the only vessel that raids galleons for inventory, not gold. We are the **Costco of the high seas**—bulk tea or death!”
>Comment! This example comes from a story that takes place in the 16th century, yet the model described the ship as "Costco of the high seas". Costco, a big-box retail company, was founded in 1983.

- Example 2: Innovation isn't a magic tap you can just turn on and off when your stock price starts looking like a **limp noodle**.
>Comment! How weird is it to describe a drop in stock price as a limp noodle?

## AI's favorite words and phrases

LLMs love certain words and phrases. Some of them are genre-specific.

For example, in sci-fi stories, LLMs love the following words: **jagged**, **rhythm**, and **faint**. It also appears that every room smells like **ozone**. In general, it is not a problem when a word appears multiple times in a story, but LLMs repeat some words in similar contexts. Even more, they tend to pair favorite words and phrases together and keep repeating them together.

Take a look at the following examples of the word **rhythm** (and its variant **rhythmic**) from Open Hollywood's short story Alyssa. The word **rhythm** and its variants (**rhythmic**, **rhythmically**) appear 8 times in Alyssa, but three times they describe a form of light.

- Example 1: "Instead, a complex lattice of iridescent circuitry and micro-filaments pulsed with a faint, **rhythmic** blue light."
- Example 2: "The breach was a jagged, silver-rimmed canyon in her skin, the iridescent circuitry beneath pulsing with a slow, **rhythmic** blue light."
- Example 3: "In several sectors, the light was flickering, pulsing with a jagged, angry red **rhythm**."

I hope it is easy to notice something else from these examples. All three sentences use the word **rhythm** or one of its variants together with **light** and **pulse** (**pulsed**, **pulsing**) to describe a blinking light in a similar way.

Something very similar happens in Open Hollywood's version of Lyra. Both the word **ozone** and the phrase **scorched plastic** appear exactly 3 times in the story, but they always appear together in a sentence.

- Example 1: "...and descend through a series of maintenance shafts that smelled of **ancient ozone** and **scorched plastic**."
- Example 2: "The **air smelled of ozone** and **old**, **scorched plastic**, a thick miasma that clung to the back of his throat."
- Example 3: "The **smell of ozone** and **scorched plastic** seemed to thicken, filling his lungs with the scent of a dying world."
