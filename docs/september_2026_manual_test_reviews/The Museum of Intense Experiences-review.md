## How strongly does this read as an AI-generated story?

`The Museum of Intense Experiences` reads strongly as an AI-generated story. In the very first sentence, our writer uses a very common scene description pattern seen in LLM-generated stories. This pattern often uses **smell**, **taste** or **scent** followed by sentence modifiers.

`The Gallery of Resonance smelled of floor wax and expensive, filtered air.`

Throughout the story, our writer repeats many of the common LLM writing tics. We can find contrastive framing over 25 times.

>Note! Below are samples, not the full list of AI tics found in The Museum of Intense Experiences.

**Contrastive framing**:

`The Museum of Intense Experiences` features some contrastive framing that I find acceptable. For example, in human speech, if not abused, contrastive framing is okay. One such example is in scene 1, when Curator Vane says: "They forget that resonance is not an intellectual
exercise; it is a frequency."

- Scene 1: "**Michael didn't feel pity**. **He felt a sudden, violent alignment**."
- Scene 2: "**The impact was not a fall**, **but a collision with a wall of liquid filth**."
- Scene 2: "But as he stepped forward, **the world didn't open**. **It snapped**."
- Scene 5: "He felt the air on his skin **not as a temperature reading**, **but as a caress**; he felt the silence **not as a void**, **but as a presence**."

**Quirks and questionable decisions**:

- Scene 2: "He navigated by instinct, dodging a spray of crimson as a soldier nearby was cleaved open from shoulder to waist. Michael didn't look back; he focused on the gray mist of the horizon. He spent what felt like hours traversing the sludge, his muscles screaming, his lungs burning. Every time a blade swung near him, he calculated the trajectory, stepping back with a precise, desperate timing. **He treated the battle like a puzzle, a lethal game of spatial awareness. He avoided the dying, avoided the killers, and kept his gaze fixed on the edge of the carnage.**"
>Comment! We know Michael got drawn into the battle and is trying to stay alive. If we want to continue showing how Michael avoided dying, then we should do that. If not, we should drop the last two sentences because they feel like a summary.

- Scene 2: "Michael scrambled backward, his hands sinking into the viscous mud, but the panic was no longer fragmented. It was a cold, hard realization. **He had run to the edge of the world, and the world had simply folded back upon itself. He was not a visitor in a painting; he was a component of it. He was trapped in the loop of a dead man's agony, and no amount of analytical distance could navigate a way out of a circle.**"
>Comment! This paragraph ends Scene 2 and it feels like a summary in a documentary series that runs several episodes.

- Scene 3: "**He didn't try to stand**. **He didn't try to calculate the perimeter**. **He simply lay there**, gasping, until he felt the familiar, rhythmic rattle of the dying man beside him."
>Comment! This is a weird mix of sibling framing (a twin) and contrastive framing. But what bothers me more is that our writer keeps telling us what our main character is not doing. 

- Scene 3: "**He didn't see a painting**. **He didn't see a data point**. **He saw the reflection** of his own lifelong isolation, magnified by a thousand deaths."
>Comment! Another example of sibling framing (a twin) and contrastive framing. Our writer, again, tells us what our main character is not doing.

## Opinion

>Note! The following opinion is for The Museum of Intense Experiences as it was outlined in Open Hollywood's pre-production and later created in production after blind human approval.

`The Museum of Intense Experiences` is a decent story. It starts well: we meet our main character, Michael Langley, in the museum and experience the museum in a way that lets us learn a few things about our protagonist. One thing in the first scene that caught my eye was this sentence:

`"You find the collection lacking in impact, Mr. Langley?"`. 

It is very unusual, perhaps almost impossible, for a museum curator to know visitors' last names. Even if Michael were carrying a name tag, the curator probably would not have done this unless they somehow knew each other.

It is the parts of the story on the battlefield that failed to turn this into a really interesting story. Nothing really happens to Michael on the battlefield. He sees death, but he never actually has a near-death experience. He never truly freaks out. A modern man drawn into a 14th-century battle would definitely freak out.

One thing our writer could've explored is this: a rogue enemy spots Michael and wants to kill him. How about that for an impact? Remember, Michael said this in scene 1, in response to Vane's question regarding the "lack of impact":

`"Not lacking," Michael replied, his voice flat. "Just... distant. It's as if I'm reading a description of a feeling rather than feeling it."`

If there were just somebody trying to kill him, then he would definitely "feel" it. Michael would then need to find creative ways to stay alive. This chase could've led him to Sir Alric, and then Alric would help Michael return to the present.

The ending could also benefit from a few changes. Once Michael returned to the present day, he would've likely looked at Curator Vane a bit differently. Their conversation in scene 1 strongly suggests Vane played a part in Michael's journey to the 14th century. There are a number of ways this could play out. The way our writer chose is likely the most sterile ending.

`The Museum of Intense Experiences` is a story that had much more potential than it shows. Unfortunately, the story doesn't have a strong villain, and the depth of our main character is not fully explored.
